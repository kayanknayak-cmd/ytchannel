# Scripts, batch 1 (10 videos)

Format: second person, viewer is the scientist. ~90-110 words (30-45s at ElevenLabs pace). Each ends on a line that flows into its opening (loop).
Defaults chosen without owner input: no recurring named character (the viewer is "you"); every video loops.

## 01 The Pigeon Box
You're a scientist, and you've got a pigeon in a box. Peck the button, get food. Simple. It eats, gets full, stops pecking. Boring. So you change one rule. Now food comes at random. Sometimes after two pecks. Sometimes after fifty. And the pigeon goes nuts. It pecks harder and longer than when the food was guaranteed. You've found the most addictive reward there is: maybe. Casinos found it too. Every slot machine on Earth runs on your random rule. Lights, sounds, a lever. And the one pulling it? That's not a pigeon. That's you. And you're the scientist now. So, you've got a pigeon in a box...
Facts: Skinner, variable-ratio schedules produce high, persistent responding; Skinner himself compared it to gambling. Slot machines predate Skinner (1890s), so script says casinos "found it too," not that they copied him.

## 02 The Wrong Line
You're a scientist, and you show a guy three lines. Which one matches this one? Easy. It's C. A kid could do it. But you've planted seven actors in the room, and every single one says A. Out loud. One by one. Now it's his turn. He stares at the lines. He knows it's C. And he says... A. You run it over and over. About three out of four people cave at least once. Not because they can't see. Because they can't stand being the only one. So next time a whole room agrees with you, ask yourself: which line is it really? Because you're a scientist, and you show a guy three lines...
Facts: Asch, 1951. ~75% conformed on at least one critical trial; ~1/3 of critical responses conformed overall.

## 03 The Button
You're a scientist, and you hand a volunteer a button. Every time the man in the next room gets a question wrong, press it. He gets a shock. Every miss, the voltage goes up. Soon he's yelling to stop. The volunteer turns to you. You just say: please continue. Here's the secret. The man is an actor. The shocks are fake. The only real thing is the volunteer's finger. Before you started, experts guessed almost nobody would go to the end. Two out of three did. All the way to the top switch. They weren't monsters. Someone in a lab coat just told them to keep going. So, who's telling you to keep going? You're a scientist, and you hand a volunteer a button...
Facts: Milgram, 1961-63, baseline: 26 of 40 (65%) to 450V. Psychiatrists predicted ~0.1%. Learner was a confederate; no real shocks. PG: no injury detail.

## 04 The Marshmallow
You're a scientist, and you put a marshmallow in front of a four year old. Wait fifteen minutes, you get two. Then you leave. Some kids eat it instantly. Some lick it. Some sit on their hands. Years later, you check on them. The kids who waited? Better test scores. The whole world loses it. Self control is destiny! Then other scientists run it again with way more kids. And once they account for things like family income, the effect mostly shrinks away. Maybe the kids who ate it just weren't sure you'd ever come back. Would you trust a stranger with your marshmallow? You're a scientist, and you put a marshmallow in front of a four year old...
Facts: Mischel, Stanford, late 1960s-70s; follow-ups linked waiting to SAT scores. Watts, Duncan & Quan 2018 (~900 kids): association about half as large, largely gone after controls. Kidd et al. 2013: kids wait less when the adult proved unreliable.

## 05 The Prison
You're a scientist, and you build a fake prison in a basement. You flip a coin. Half your volunteers are guards. Half are prisoners. The plan is two weeks. By day six, it's so out of control you shut it down. The lesson goes in every textbook: give normal people power and they turn cruel. Except, decades later, people dig up the recordings. And the guards were being coached. Told to be tough. Some said they were acting. It wasn't really the power. It was the guy running the show. Which, in this story... is you. You're a scientist, and you build a fake prison in a basement...
Facts: Zimbardo, Stanford Prison Experiment, Aug 1971; planned 14 days, ended after 6. Le Texier (2018) and released recordings show guards were encouraged toward harshness; some guards said they acted a role. Assignment by coin flip per Zimbardo.

## 06 The Smoke
You're a scientist, and you sit a student alone in a room to fill out a form. Then you pump smoke through the vent. Most of them get up fast and go report it. Now you do it again. Same smoke. But this time there are two other people in the room, and they're in on it. They see the smoke. They shrug. They keep writing. And the student? Usually sits there. Coughing. Writing. Waiting for someone else to move. Alone, about three in four reported it. With the two calm actors, one in ten. Everyone's waiting for someone else. So be the someone. Before you're the one sitting in the smoke. You're a scientist...
Facts: Latane & Darley, 1968. Alone: 75% reported within 6 minutes. With two passive confederates: 10%. Smoke was harmless.

## 07 The Gorilla
You're a scientist, and you show people a video of players passing a basketball. Count the passes by the team in white. That's it. Halfway through, a person in a full gorilla suit walks into the middle. Stops. Beats its chest. Walks off. Afterwards, you ask: did you notice anything weird? About half of them say no. They missed a gorilla. When you focus hard on one thing, your brain just deletes the rest. Did you count the passes? Good. Then you probably missed what I'm about to show you. You're a scientist...
Facts: Simons & Chabris, 1999, "Gorillas in our midst"; ~50% failed to notice. Gorilla thumps chest, ~9 seconds on screen.

## 08 The Jam Table
You're a scientist, and you set up a jam table in a grocery store. Day one: twenty four flavors. Huge crowd. Day two: just six. Smaller crowd. But here's the weird part. With six, about ten times more people actually buy one. Too many choices and people freeze, then walk away with nothing. The story goes viral. Every company starts cutting options. But when other scientists try it again, sometimes the effect shows up, sometimes it vanishes. Turns out it depends on who's shopping and what they're choosing. So what's the lesson? Pick one. Even this one. Because you're a scientist, and you set up a jam table...
Facts: Iyengar & Lepper, 2000: 24 vs 6 jams; 60% vs 40% stopped; 3% vs 30% bought. Scheibehenne et al. 2010 meta-analysis: mean effect near zero, highly variable.

## 09 The Sermon
You're a scientist, and you ask some priests in training to give a talk. Some get assigned the story of the Good Samaritan. The guy who stops to help a stranger. Then you send them across campus. On the way, they pass a man slumped in a doorway, groaning. Your actor. Who stops? It barely matters what speech they're carrying. What matters is one thing you said: you're late, hurry. When they had time, most stopped. When they were rushing, about one in ten. Some literally stepped over him. On the way to talk about helping people. So, how late are you right now? You're a scientist...
Facts: Darley & Batson, 1973, Princeton Theological Seminary. Low hurry 63% helped, high hurry 10%. Topic had no significant effect. Report notes some subjects stepped over the victim.

## 10 The Mirror
You're a scientist, and you put out a bowl of Halloween candy. Take one, you tell the kids. Then you leave the room. A lot of them grab a handful. Now you do it again, but you put a mirror right behind the bowl. Same candy. Same empty room. The only difference: they can see themselves. And suddenly, way fewer cheat. Nobody was watching. Except the kid in the mirror. Turns out we behave better when we can see who we're being. Look at your screen. See your reflection? Now you're a scientist...
Facts: Beaman, Klentz, Diener & Svanum, 1979, trick-or-treaters, "take one" instruction, experimenter leaves. Mirror reduced taking extra among kids who were asked their names (roughly 37% to 9%). VERIFY exact figures before producing.
